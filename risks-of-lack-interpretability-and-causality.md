# Risks of lack of interpretability and causality in AI models

Although the performance of deep neural networks is impressive, the lack of interpretability can have catastrophic results when the issues of high reliability and ethics are not addressed. In terms of reliability and model auditing, a false negative (incorrectly indicating that a condition is absent) may not be a big issue in spam detection. However, when it comes to self-driving cars, reliable image recognition of a human as a human is vital (Dickson, 2020).
In terms of ethics, a deep neural network may adopt biases inherent in the collected training set. For example, a neural network may discover that an effective way to maximize profits would be to engage in predatory behavior (Hao, 2020).
After reading these articles, I wanted to know if there are any other examples and how could it affect any application that could be use in my everydat life.

## Examples of AI systems that have produced negative outcomes due to a lack of interpretability and learned lessons

In 2021, the Dutch tax authorities used a risk-classification AI model to identify incorrect or fraudulent applications in the childcare-benefit program. With that response as a base, people who were flagged could face investigations, suspension of benefits, and aggressive recovery procedures performed by the authorities. When the system was being used, the reviewers of the outcome that confirmed the flag were not told why a case generated a high-risk score, leaving them without enough information to make a good decision. However, most of the reviewers took the AI responses as the truth and didn’t notice that the AI took the nationality of the applicants as a risk factor, with non-Dutch nationals always receiving higher risk scores. This finally led to a huge scandal in the EU because discrimination patterns were being replicated throughout the AI without auditability. The lesson we could take from this example is that adding a human in the loop does not automatically mean that we are creating useful human oversight, because the people in charge of these activities need to have the complete information to take such delicate decisions in high-impact systems. In this case, the lack of interpretability and ethics of the AI, added to other policies and administrative failures, led to discriminatory practices which could have been detected earlier if the reviewers got all the information from the model.

The case of Uber in 2018 is an example I heard of some time ago where an automated driving vehicle crashed in Tempe, Arizona. According to the US National Transportation Safety Board, the driving system detected the affected pedestrian 5.6 seconds before the crash correctly but never classified her as a pedestrian or predicted her path. However, the investigations revealed that the lack of interpretability of the neural network was only part of the causes, because the main probable reason was the safety driver’s failure to monitor the road. Also, other contributing factors detected were Uber’s inadequate safety-risk procedures and inadequate handling of automation complacency. The case shows us that interpretability should also exist at the system level, not just inside the neural network. Engineers should be able to reconstruct the information to show the change in classifications over time, how uncertain the system was, and why safety responses were not triggered. For this kind of system and with little confidence in the classification or the path prediction, the model should have moved to a safer state instead of continuing the operation to avoid any accident. At that moment, it was huge news; however, nowadays the lesson is learned, and safety procedures are a default for IoT systems to protect people from possible accidents.

## Possible applications of neural networks in my life and how lack of interpretability may be an issue in terms of reliability or ethics

With the boom of AI, there are several applications that I could think of; I will mention 5 possible applications and how the lack of interpretability could be an issue. The applications I will talk about are automated essay assessments, personalized article/news recommendations, project effort and cost predictions, AI-assisted code generation and review, and video-based techniques and pose analysis in sports.

For the first case, teachers could use CNN and transformer models to evaluate written work such as essays from their students. While the model would be very useful for teachers, they need accurate information about how the grade was determined in terms of reasoning, organization, evidence, and language. If that interpretability is lacking, student preferences for vocabulary or writing styles could create unfair results without the teachers noticing it. To address it, the teachers and the data team should ask themselves: Are certain words improving quality, or are they correlated with historically high-scoring essays?

The second case happens in social media, where recommendation systems use your web interaction information to show you articles or news that will probably be of interest to you. Personally, as a user, I would like to know why an article or news is being recommended to me repeatedly, narrowing the information that I read. Also, my reading history is personal behavioural data that should not be used unethically to show me only focused information.

Estimation of project effort and cost done by CNN is currently applied by many software companies; however, these estimates, often black-box ones, would impact staffing, budgets, deadlines, and expectations for a team. As a project manager, I would want to know which were the main drivers for the decision and the level of uncertainty around the estimate. For example, I would want the system to tell me if the decision to make a project more difficult was because larger teams historically appear on difficult projects or if it has another reason to consider.

For AI-assisted code generation and review, transformers can generate programs from natural-language descriptions; however, this generated code can look reasonable while also containing logical or security errors. I would personally treat the model output as a proposal that requires validation, analysis, and security reviews. The reason for this is that a code pattern that appears frequently for training tests does not make it correct or secure for the particular architecture I am working with. It is better to be sure before using any generated code in production than having issues later.

Finally, video-based techniques and pose analysis performed by CNN models could be used by coaches to provide feedback to the athletes. However, incorrect joint detection in the models could lead to incorrect advice. If I were an athlete or a coach, I would like to see the detected joints in an interpretable way (like an image), the confidence levels of the model, and which frames were too problematic for the model to evaluate. With this information, I could make better decisions and know if one specific movement would help me improve or if there could be other biomechanical factors that contribute to the improvement.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, I would say that the higher the consequence, the less comfortable I would be with accuracy as the only measure of quality for an AI recommendation. For light consequences, the result might be only annoying, like the example of articles or news recommendations. However, for project decisions, autonomous vehicles, or public benefits, I would expect stronger requirements for interpretability and auditing because the decisions made with these results will affect people in different ways, sometimes very dangerous ones.
